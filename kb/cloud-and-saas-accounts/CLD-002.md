# CLD-002 · A tested break-glass path and business-owned recovery contacts exist for every critical account

## Why it matters
Strong sign-in rules create a new risk: the business can lock itself out. The one admin who holds the only security key can lose it, leave, fall ill or be unreachable, and the company then cannot change DNS, pay the cloud bill or reset anyone's access. The opposite mistake is just as common: the account owner, billing contact and recovery email are the founder's personal mailbox and mobile number, so whoever controls that mailbox controls the company account. A planned emergency route, kept offline and watched, solves both problems.

## How to test
For each critical service (email suite, DNS and CDN, registrar, code host, cloud host, payment provider) record who can sign in as owner, and where the account-level contacts point.
1. Count owners. Google Workspace: Directory, Users, filter by admin role, or Account, Admin roles. Microsoft 365: Entra admin center, Roles and administrators, Global Administrator. Cloudflare: Manage Account, Members, look for more than one Super Administrator. GitHub: organisation People, filter role Owner.
2. Read the contact fields: billing contact, account owner email, recovery email and phone, security contact. Each should be a role mailbox on a domain you control or a named second person, not a single person's personal address. Do not make the recovery mailbox live on the same domain that the account itself protects without a second route.
3. Find the break-glass account: a dedicated admin account not tied to a person, with a long random password and a hardware key, whose credentials sit in a sealed envelope or safe. In Microsoft Entra check it is cloud-only, has the Global Administrator role as a permanent active assignment, and is excluded from sign-in-blocking Conditional Access policies.
4. For AWS, check the root user is not used day to day: `aws iam get-account-summary --query 'SummaryMap.[AccountMFAEnabled,AccountAccessKeysPresent]'` should print `1` and `0`.
5. Ask whether anyone has tried the emergency path in the last year, and whether any use of the account would raise an alert (see CLD-007).

## Pass criteria
Every critical service has at least two people who can act as owner, and a documented break-glass route whose credentials are stored offline in a place more than one authorised person can reach. Billing, owner and recovery contacts are role mailboxes or named deputies on business-controlled domains. The route is tested at least once a year (Microsoft's guidance is every 90 days for its emergency accounts), any sign-in with it raises an alert, and on AWS the root user has MFA and no access keys.

## Fix
Create the second owner and the break-glass account, register their hardware keys, seal the credentials in two separate places, and write a one-page procedure that says who may open them and what to do afterwards (rotate the credentials, review the log). Change contact fields to role mailboxes such as `it@` and `billing@`. Add the drill to the yearly calendar.

## Risk and rollback
A break-glass account is a standing powerful credential, so it must be watched and used only for emergencies; an unwatched one is a back door. Changing the account owner or billing email at some providers triggers a verification or a waiting period; do it during working hours with both old and new mailboxes accessible. Rollback: the previous contact values can be put back from the notes you took before the change.
