# IR-002 · One incident lead and a deputy are named and have written authority to isolate systems

## Why it matters
Containment often means taking something offline that customers use: a website, the mail server, a payment page. If nobody is clearly allowed to make that call, people wait for the owner to answer a phone, and the attacker keeps working. Guidance for small and medium organisations asks that responsible people be named in the plan. In a business of 1 to 50 people the roles can be shared, but the decision rights must still be explicit, including who is on duty when the lead is travelling or asleep. Where personal information is involved, Quebec's Law 25 also expects the person in charge of the protection of personal information to take part in the risk assessment, so that person should have a defined place in the plan.

## How to test
1. Ask: "Suppose it is 22:00 on a Saturday and the mail server is sending spam. Who decides to shut it down, and who do they tell?" Write down each answer separately from three people (owner, technical person, one other employee). Compare.
2. Read the plan or policy for: the incident lead, a deputy, a technical responder (internal or on retainer), a communications owner, and the person in charge of personal information.
3. Confirm that the lead's authority to isolate or switch off a system without further approval is written down and signed or acknowledged by the owner.
4. Check that the technical responder actually holds the access needed (hosting console, registrar, DNS, mail admin, cloud root or break-glass account), and that at least two people can reach each.

## Pass criteria
Roles are named with a deputy for each. Authority to contain (isolate a host, disable an account, take a site offline) is written, with an expected-impact threshold above which the owner must still be told. Three independent answers to the 22:00 question match. Access to critical consoles does not depend on a single person.

## Fix
Add a half-page "who decides" table to the plan: incident lead, deputy, technical responder, communications owner, privacy lead, executive sponsor, each with a backup. State plainly that the lead may disconnect or shut down any system when there is credible evidence of compromise, and must inform the owner within a set time (for example 30 minutes). Arrange access sharing through a password manager with an emergency access feature, and keep registrar and cloud root accounts under at least two named people.

## Risk and rollback
Giving staff the power to shut things down can cause avoidable outages if used on weak evidence. Counter this with a short triage question list (IR-004) and a review after each use. Rollback: if the authority proves too broad, narrow it in writing to named systems rather than removing it.
