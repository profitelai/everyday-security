# IR-004 · Security alerts reach a person, staff know how to report a suspected incident, and severity is ranked with a simple scale

## Why it matters
Most incidents are first noticed by a human: a customer reports spam from your domain, an employee clicks something odd, a site shows strange content, the host sends an abuse notice. If nobody knows where to report it, the report waits. Triage then decides whether an event is a real incident and how urgent it is; without a scale, everything is either ignored or treated as a disaster. The lifecycle guidance puts detection and analysis, including documenting events and deciding whether to activate the plan, as its own phase.

## How to test
1. List the signals you rely on: uptime monitor, mail-queue or bounce alerts, host abuse notices, security alerts from cloud and SaaS providers (new login, new admin, forwarding rule, API key created), endpoint or antivirus alerts, backup failure alerts, WAF or fail2ban notices. For each, find the destination mailbox or chat channel and confirm a human reads it, including out of hours and during holidays.
2. Trigger a harmless test where possible (for example a test alert from the monitoring tool, or a forwarded sample abuse report) and time how long until a named person acknowledges it.
3. Ask three employees: "You think your account was hacked. Who do you tell, how, and what do you do while waiting?" Compare the answers with the plan.
4. Read the severity scale. Check that it defines two to four levels by business impact (for example: single user with no data exposed; one system affected; customer data or money possibly exposed; business stopped), with an expected response time for each and a rule for when the privacy lead joins.
5. Check that a single report channel exists (a shared mailbox and a phone number) and that reporting in good faith is explicitly safe.

## Pass criteria
Each important signal goes to a place a person actually monitors, and the test alert was acknowledged within the time written in the plan. Staff give the same, correct answer on how to report. A short severity scale exists with response times. There is an incident log (even a spreadsheet) in which every report is recorded with time, reporter, first assessment and decision.

## Fix
Create one shared reporting mailbox and phone line, publish it to staff, and state that reporting a mistake is welcome. Route alerts to a mailbox that forwards to two people and to a phone-visible channel. Write a four-level severity table of half a page. Open an incident log from today, and record near-misses too. Any incident involving personal information also goes into the confidentiality incident register (see PRIV-006).

## Risk and rollback
More alerts can cause fatigue; tune thresholds so the channel stays quiet enough to be believed. Do not route alerts only to the mail server being monitored. Rollback: if a rule floods the channel, mute that one rule after recording why, rather than silencing the whole channel.
