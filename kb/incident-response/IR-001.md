# IR-001 · A short written incident response plan exists and can be opened when systems are down

## Why it matters
Without a plan, the first hours of an incident are spent arguing about who is in charge and what to try first, and mistakes made in that time (wiping a server, emailing the attacker's mailbox, deleting logs) are hard to undo. Public guidance from NIST and the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security describes the same lifecycle: prepare, detect and analyse, contain, eradicate, recover, and learn. A small business does not need a thick document, but it needs one that people can find and follow under stress. A plan stored only on the file server or in the email system you are trying to recover is unreachable when you need it most.

## How to test
1. Ask the owner for the plan. Time how long it takes to produce it.
2. Check that it covers, at minimum: what counts as an incident, who decides (IR-002), who to call (IR-003), how an incident is reported and ranked (IR-004), first containment steps and evidence handling (IR-005, IR-010), who speaks to customers and regulators (IR-007), and recovery and review (IR-008).
3. Find where copies are kept. Look for at least one copy that does not depend on your own servers, mail or SSO (a printed copy, a phone, a cloud drive under a different account).
4. Check the date of the last review and whether the contact details in it are current.
5. Ask two staff members where the plan is. A plan nobody can locate is the same as no plan.

## Pass criteria
A written plan of roughly two to ten pages exists, is dated within the last 12 months, covers the topics above, names real people and roles rather than job titles alone, and is stored in at least one place that stays reachable if your servers, mail and primary cloud account are down. At least two people besides the author know where it is.

## Fix
Start from a free outline (the Cyber Centre's guidance on developing an incident response plan, the CISA plan factsheet, or the NIST lifecycle) and shrink it to the scenarios that fit you: lost or stolen laptop, compromised mailbox, defaced or infected website, ransomware, leaked customer data, compromised cloud account. Keep it plain and short. Print one copy for the office and store one with a person outside the business who can hand it over. Put a yearly review in the calendar.

## Risk and rollback
Writing a plan is low risk. The risks are a plan that goes stale, or one that holds sensitive details such as passwords (do not put credentials in it; point to the password manager and the break-glass process instead). Rollback: if a plan version turns out wrong, supersede it with a dated new version and collect the old printed copies.
