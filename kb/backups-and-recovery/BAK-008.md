# BAK-008 · Recovery objectives (RPO and RTO) are documented, with a short recovery runbook

## Why it matters
Two numbers drive every backup decision. The recovery point objective (RPO) is how much recent data you can afford to lose, which sets how often you must back up. The recovery time objective (RTO) is how long the service may stay down, which sets how fast restores must be. Without them, schedules and storage are guesses, and during an outage people lose time working out what to do first.

## How to test
Ask for these documents and read them:
1. For each service: its RPO and RTO, agreed with whoever owns the business impact.
2. Compare to reality. If the RPO is one hour but backups run nightly, or the RTO is two hours but the last restore test took six, that is a gap.
3. A runbook: who declares an incident, where credentials and keys are, in what order systems come back, how to restore each one, how to check it worked, and who to tell.
4. The runbook is reachable when production is down (a printed or off-site copy, not only on the failed server).

## Pass criteria
RPO and RTO exist for each important service, and measured backup frequency and restore time meet them. A runbook of a few pages covers the steps above, has been followed in a restore test (BAK-005), and has an owner and a review date.

## Fix
Write the numbers down per service, in plain terms with the business owner. Match the backup frequency and restore method to them. Write the runbook from your most recent restore drill, and store a copy outside the systems it describes. Review it after every significant change.

## Risk and rollback
A strict RPO or RTO costs more (more frequent backups, standby capacity), so set them from impact, not wishful thinking. A runbook with secrets in it is itself a target; keep credentials in the password manager and only point to them. Rollback of a document change is restoring the previous version.
